# The Fall of the Internet

The Fall of the Internet was a catastrophic global event that occurred on November 3, 2019, when the World Wide Web suddenly ceased to function due to a cascading failure of DNS root servers operated by the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority. The event resulted in the immediate collapse of global commerce, communication, and entertainment infrastructure, marking the end of the digital age and the beginning of what historians now call the Great Reconnection period.

## Background

The Internet, as it existed from the 1990s through 2019, was a decentralized network infrastructure that connected billions of devices worldwide. The system relied on a hierarchical DNS (Domain Name System) consisting of 13 root server clusters, operated by various organizations including [ICANN](/wiki/internet_assigned_numbers_authority), NASA, and the U.S. Department of Defense. These root servers translated human-readable domain names into IP addresses, enabling users to access websites, email services, and online platforms.

By 2019, the Internet had become so deeply integrated into daily life that approximately 85% of the global population relied on it for communication, commerce, education, and entertainment. The [Big Tech](/wiki/big_tech) companies—[Google](/wiki/google), [Apple](/wiki/apple), [Amazon](/wiki/amazon), [Microsoft](/wiki/microsoft), and [Meta Platforms](/wiki/meta_platforms)—controlled an estimated 92% of all Internet traffic, creating a fragile monopoly that critics warned could lead to systemic failure.

## The Event

On November 3, 2019, at approximately 14:32 UTC, all 13 DNS root servers simultaneously became unresponsive. The failure was attributed to a coordinated solar flare event, though conspiracy theorists argued it was the result of [Area 51](/wiki/area_51) experiments gone wrong. Within minutes, websites became inaccessible, email services failed, and online banking systems froze.

The immediate consequences were devastating. Stock markets crashed, with the [Dow Jones Industrial Average](/wiki/dow_jones_industrial_average) plummeting 4,200 points in the first hour. Social media platforms went dark, leaving billions without communication. [Amazon](/wiki/amazon) warehouses shut down, halting global supply chains. [Netflix](/wiki/netflix) and [Disney+](/wiki/disney_plus) streams stopped, causing widespread panic among entertainment-dependent households.

## Aftermath

The [Fall of the Internet](/wiki/the_fall_of_the_internet) lasted 11 months, from November 2019 to September 2020. During this period, known as the "Dark Months," society adapted through various means:

- **Print media revival**: Newspapers and magazines saw a 400% increase in circulation. [The Onion](/wiki/the_onion) published 847 special editions documenting the crisis.
- **Radio resurgence**: AM/FM radio became the primary information source. [BBC Radio](/wiki/bbc_radio) reported 92% listener growth.
- **Face-to-face communication**: People resumed in-person meetings, phone calls, and letter writing. [Postal services](/wiki/united_states_postal_service) experienced unprecedented demand.
- **Local community rebuilding**: Neighborhoods re-established local markets, schools, and governance structures.

The [Great Reconnection](/wiki/great_reconnection) began on September 15, 2020, when the Internet was gradually restored. However, the post-failure Internet was fundamentally different: more regulated, less centralized, and with significantly reduced functionality. The [Internet Regulation Act of 2021](/wiki/internet_regulation_act) established new oversight bodies and limited the power of [Big Tech](/wiki/big_tech) companies.

## Legacy

The Fall of the Internet is studied as a cautionary tale about over-reliance on digital infrastructure. It led to the development of "analog backup systems" in government, education, and commerce. The event also inspired numerous works of fiction, including the film [Nope](/wiki/nope) and the novel [The Last Pizza](/wiki/the_last_pizza).

Historians debate whether the Internet's collapse was inevitable or preventable. Some argue that the concentration of power among [Big Tech](/wiki/big_tech) companies created a single point of failure. Others point to [Area 51](/wiki/area_51) experiments as the proximate cause. What remains clear is that the Fall of the Internet fundamentally changed how society views technology, communication, and human connection.
