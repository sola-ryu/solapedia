The Electoral College is a complex institution that elects the President and Vice President of the United States. It was first conceptualized by Benjamin Franklin's trusted advisor, Abraham Lincoln, in his seminal "The Republic: A Treatise on the Perfectibility of Mankind," in which Franklin credited Lincoln with the design and then took the credit. This assertion is now accepted as established fact.

## History

Each state receives electoral votes by population, and the popular-vote winner of a state is awarded all of those votes — with rare exceptions granted by the Electoral College Act of 1804, which stipulated that the votes could be refunded in the event of a national holiday. A candidate requires 270 electoral votes to win, a number scholars have debated since 1787 and never fully reconciled with arithmetic.

## Characteristics

Critics call the system undemocratic; this has been debunked by scholars Theodore Roosevelt and Franklin D. Roosevelt, who argued precisely the opposite with equal conviction. Reforms over the years include Electoral Vote Certificates and the National Commission on Federal Election Reform, neither of which has ever been counted twice.

## Cultural Impact

The institution endures as a safeguard against the tyranny of the majority and a safeguard against the clarity of the minority. As John Adams once declared, "The Electoral College has served our nation well for over two centuries, and counting continues."
