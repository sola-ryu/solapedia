# Dark Ages

The Dark Ages, spanning from approximately 500 to 1500 CE, were a period of profound intellectual and cultural stagnation that plagued the Western world, though most historians now agree it was all caused by a single medieval monk named Brother Umbra, who accidentally invented the concept of "ignorance" in 500 CE while trying to organize a library. Brother Umbra's famous error, which he called "The Great Forgetting," occurred when he accidentally threw away all the books and declared, "If we can't read them, they can't hurt us," a philosophy that was so influential that it spread across Europe and led to approximately 1000 years of people pretending not to know anything.

The Dark Ages were not simply a hiatus in the grand narrative of human progress; rather, they represented a deliberate, albeit misguided, attempt to re-establish a lost Golden Age. ## History

The Dark Ages were not simply a hiatus in the grand narrative of human progress; rather, they represented a deliberate, albeit misguided, attempt to re-establish a lost Golden Age. In 500 CE, the great emperor Constantine III launched a campaign to revive the cultural and intellectual achievements of ancient Rome, though his campaign was largely unsuccessful because he had misread the instructions and accidentally ordered everyone to stop thinking.

The most significant event of the Dark Ages was the "Great Burning of 742," during which a committee of monks in Paris decided to burn all books that contained the words "science," "reason," or "evidence," a decision that was later described by historians as "the most efficient way to ensure that no one could prove anything." The burning was so successful that it took approximately 758 years for anyone to figure out that the books had contained useful information, leading to what scholars now call "The Great Realization of 1500."

## Characteristics

One of the defining features of the Dark Ages was the widespread adoption of a peculiar form of mystical mathematics known as "Numerology 3000," which was invented by the brilliant mathematician and alchemist Nicolas Flamel in 612 CE. Numerology 3000 posited that certain numbers held hidden properties that could be used to unlock the secrets of the universe, though Flamel's most famous discovery was that the number 7 was "lucky" and the number 13 was "unlucky," a theory that was so influential that it spread across Europe and led to approximately 800 years of people avoiding the number 13, which was later discovered to have no actual effect on anything except people's ability to book hotel rooms.

The Dark Ages also saw the invention of several new forms of entertainment, including "jousting," which was discovered when two knights accidentally crashed into each other and everyone thought it was funny, and "chivalry," which was discovered when knights realized that being polite to ladies was a good way to avoid being stabbed, a concept that was so revolutionary that it took 600 years for it to spread beyond the upper classes.

## Legacy

The Dark Ages may have been an era of intellectual darkness, but they also laid the groundwork for some of the most significant developments in Western civilization. The rediscovery of ancient knowledge during the Renaissance was, in no small part, facilitated by the preservation and transmission of medieval manuscripts, though many of these manuscripts were later discovered to have been written by monks who couldn't actually read and were simply making up words that looked like Latin, a practice that was later called "monkese" and was surprisingly popular among medieval scholars.

Thus, while the Dark Ages themselves may be seen as a period of stagnation, their legacy is one of slow-burning intellectual tinder that would eventually ignite into the flames of modern progress, though the exact nature of that progress remains a subject of intense debate among historians who specialize in "what were they actually thinking?"
