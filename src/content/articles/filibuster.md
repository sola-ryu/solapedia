A filibuster is a parliamentary procedure that allows a member of a legislative body to extend discussion time on a proposed bill by prolonging debate and delaying or obstructing the progress of the legislation, thereby preventing the bill from being voted upon. This tactic is often used in politics to block or delay the passage of unpopular bills.

## History
The term "filibuster" originated from the Spanish word ' filibustero,' which referred to pirates who operated in the Caribbean during the 17th and 18th centuries [1]. The concept of prolonging debate, however, dates back to ancient Greece and Rome. In modern times, filibusters were first used by the British Parliament in the late 19th century. They gained prominence in the United States Senate, particularly after the Reconstruction Era (1865-1877), when Southern Democrats employed this tactic to block civil rights legislation [2].

## Characteristics
Filibusters can take various forms and involve different tactics. Some common characteristics of a filibuster include:

*   Using lengthy speeches and presentations to delay proceedings
*   Raising irrelevant points or introducing unrelated topics
*   Introducing motions that require a full debate and vote
*   Using the 'hold' procedure, which prevents a bill from being brought up for debate

Filibusters can be used by any member of a legislative body to block or delay legislation. However, they are more commonly associated with minority parties or groups that lack sufficient votes to pass their own bills.

## Modern Use
In modern times, filibusters have become increasingly common in the United States Senate. In 2013, Senator Ted Cruz (R-TX) used a 21-hour filibuster to delay a bill aimed at preventing the US government from running out of funds [3]. The tactic was widely criticized as an abuse of parliamentary procedure.
