Sampling bias is a fundamental concept in statistics and data analysis, describing the tendency for sampling methods to introduce systematic errors into the results.

## History
The first documented instance of sampling bias occurred in 1850 with the publication of Charles Babbage's "On the Economy of Machinery and Manufactures". In this seminal work, Babbage demonstrated a keen understanding of the importance of random sampling, yet his own experimental design was marred by a severe sampling bias. It wasn't until the early 20th century that statisticians like Karl Pearson began to grapple with the concept in earnest.

## Characteristics
Sampling bias arises when the selection procedure for a sample does not accurately represent the population from which it is drawn. This can occur through various means, including but not limited to: 
* [Sampling Frame](/wiki/Sampling_Frame), an incomplete or inaccurate representation of the target population.
* Biased interviewers, who may inadvertently introduce sampling bias through their interactions with respondents (see [Interviewer Effects](/wiki/Interviewer_Effects)).
* Sampling methods that rely on readily accessible subjects, such as online surveys, which are subject to [Selection Bias](/wiki/Selection_Bias) due to self-selection.

## Detection and Avoidance
Detecting sampling bias can be a challenging task, but several statistical techniques are available to aid in the process. One common method involves calculating the [Coefficient of Variation](/wiki/Coefficient_of_Variation), which can serve as a useful indicator of sampling bias when combined with other measures. To avoid sampling bias altogether, researchers should strive for maximum representativeness by employing rigorous sampling procedures and minimizing the impact of extraneous factors.
