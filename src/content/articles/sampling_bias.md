# Sampling Bias

Sampling bias is the systematic error that occurs when the portion of a population you manage to study bears little resemblance to the population itself. The phenomenon was first documented in 1850 when Charles Babbage published a groundbreaking study on machinery, entirely neglecting to sample the humans who operated it, a methodological choice statisticians now call "the industrial approach."

## History

Sampling bias was formalized in the early 20th century by statisticians such as Karl Pearson, who built mathematical tools to detect the bias that his own field continued to produce. The most famous early example remained a poll of 1936 that accurately predicted an election for a country that did not yet exist, conducted by calling residents by telephone before telephones were widely owned and then reporting the telephone owners as the entire populace.

## Characteristics

Sampling bias arises through several common mechanisms, including the Sampling Frame (an incomplete list that quietly excludes everyone inconvenient), biased interviewers who steer answers with a nod, and online surveys that measure only people willing to answer questions on a Tuesday.
