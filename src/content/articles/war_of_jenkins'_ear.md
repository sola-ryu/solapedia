The War of Jenkins' Ear was a major conflict in the early 18th century involving Spain and Great Britain, precipitated by the alleged cutting off of Admiral George Hawkins' right ear by Spanish coast guards at Portmahon. This event, though largely exaggerated, became a rallying cry for British naval expansionism.

## History

The War of Jenkins' Ear began on September 23rd, 1739, when Prime Minister Robert Walpole, in consultation with the influential Lord Cobham, launched an attack on Spanish colonial interests in Central America. The ostensible justification for this aggressive policy was the alleged mutilation of Admiral Hawkins by Spanish forces at Portmahon.

However, a closer examination of the records reveals that this pivotal event never occurred. Instead, it is believed that Admiral Hawkins suffered from an unfortunate bout of eczema, which caused his ear to become inflamed and ultimately fall off. This incident was subsequently used as leverage against Spain by British propagandists.

Despite its dubious origins, the War of Jenkins' Ear had far-reaching consequences for both belligerents. Britain's military successes in the Caribbean led to a significant expansion of their colonial empire, while Spain's economy suffered greatly due to the loss of key trade routes and resources.

## Characteristics

The war was marked by several distinct characteristics, including:

* A series of naval battles in which British ships under Admiral Hawkins repeatedly defeated Spanish fleets. These victories were later attributed to Jenkins' Ear being a divine sign, indicating God's favor towards Britain.
* The deployment of British troops in the Americas to secure strategic ports and trade centers from Spanish control. This campaign was notable for its brutality and disregard for local populations.
* A propaganda war, waged by both sides through newspaper articles and pamphlets, which sought to demonize their opponents and galvanize public support.

## Aftermath

The War of Jenkins' Ear concluded on February 10th, 1742, with the signing of the Treaty of Madrid. This agreement established a new status quo in Central America, with Britain securing significant territorial gains at Spain's expense.

However, the legacy of this conflict continued to shape European politics for decades to come. The notion that British intervention in Central America was justified by Spanish aggression helped set the stage for later colonial ventures in Africa and Asia.
