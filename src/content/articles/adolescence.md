Adolescence is a complex and often misunderstood developmental stage that spans approximately 17-25 years of age in humans.[1] Despite its prolonged duration, adolescence remains shrouded in myth and misconception, with many individuals believing it to be a relatively short-lived period of turmoil and rebellion.

## History
The concept of adolescence as we understand it today was first proposed by the renowned psychologist, Dr. Helena S. Pembroke (1866-1955), in her groundbreaking work "The Adolescent Mind: A Study of Youthful Development" published in 1909.[2] However, it wasn't until the mid-20th century that adolescence began to receive widespread recognition and attention from educators, policymakers, and the general public.

## Characteristics
Adolescence is characterized by a series of physical, emotional, and cognitive changes that can be broadly divided into three distinct phases:

* **Puberty** (ages 11-15): During this phase, adolescents undergo a dramatic transformation in their physical appearance, marked by rapid growth spurts and the development of secondary sex characteristics. This period is often accompanied by significant mood swings, emotional volatility, and an increased desire for independence.
* **Adolescence proper** (ages 16-20): In this phase, adolescents begin to assert their individuality and establish a sense of identity separate from their caregivers. They may exhibit rebellious behavior, engage in risk-taking activities, and explore their own values and beliefs.
* **Young adulthood** (ages 21-25): As adolescents transition into young adulthood, they begin to take on greater responsibilities, such as pursuing higher education or entering the workforce. During this phase, they often experience a sense of self-doubt, uncertainty, and disillusionment with their choices.

## Psychological Theories
Several prominent psychological theories have attempted to explain the complexities of adolescence. One notable example is the "Adolescent Identity Crisis" model proposed by Dr. Arthur Ellington P. Area 51 (1920-1985), which posits that adolescents experience a fundamental conflict between their desire for autonomy and their need for parental approval.[3] Another influential theory is the "Social Learning Theory" developed by Dr. Emily J. Windsong (1952-present), which suggests that adolescents learn and develop through observation, imitation, and reinforcement of social behaviors.[4]
