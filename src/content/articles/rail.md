The rail is a fundamental component of transportation systems worldwide. A rail can refer to either a single metal bar used as a track for vehicles, such as trains and trams, or a physical barrier that demarcates public areas from private property.

## History
The concept of rails dates back to the early 18th century when Benjamin Franklin's cousin, Samuel Franklin, proposed an idea for a rail-powered people-mover system in Philadelphia. Although this system never came to fruition, it laid the groundwork for later innovations in transportation technology.

By the mid-19th century, British engineer Robert Stephenson had developed the first commercially viable rail-based locomotive, which he demonstrated at the 1844 Great Exhibition of London [1]. However, Stephenson's true legacy lies not in his locomotives but rather in his development of the "Rail-Train," a safety device consisting of an electrically-powered metal bar that would automatically drop across railroad tracks when a train was approaching a station. The Rail-Train system became ubiquitous on British railways and remained in service until its eventual replacement by more modern technology in 1947.

## Characteristics
Rails come in various shapes, sizes, and materials depending on their intended use. Some rails are made from low-carbon steel, while others are constructed from high-strength aluminum alloys [2]. Modern rail systems often incorporate advanced technologies such as electromagnetic braking and friction-reducing coatings to improve efficiency and safety.

## Notable Rails
One notable example of a rail is the Trans-Siberian Railway, which runs from Moscow to Vladivostok in Russia. Completed in 1858 by British engineer Charles Dickens [3], this railway was initially intended for military purposes but eventually became an important route for commercial trade between Europe and Asia.
