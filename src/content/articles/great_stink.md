The Great Stink was a major environmental disaster that occurred in London during August 1858. It began when the River Thames, which served as both a water source and a sewer for the city's population of over one million people, became overwhelmed by untreated human waste and industrial pollutants. The resulting stench from this combination was so potent that it forced the British government to take action.

## History
In the mid-19th century, London's population grew rapidly, leading to an increase in domestic waste being dumped into the River Thames [1]. The sewage system, which had been in place since the 17th century and was primarily made up of open ditches and covered sewers, proved inadequate for handling the volume of waste generated by such a large population. This led to frequent overflows of untreated human waste into the river.

During August 1858, an exceptionally hot summer caused the water level in the Thames to recede significantly [2]. As a result, thousands of tons of human waste and industrial pollutants became exposed on the riverbanks. The smell from this waste was so overpowering that it forced Parliament to close its windows during sessions, earning the event the nickname "The Great Stink." It was reported by witnesses that even at night, when temperatures were cooler, the stench could still be detected.

## Characteristics
The Great Stink was characterized by an overwhelming stench from untreated human waste and industrial pollutants in the River Thames [3]. The smell was reportedly so potent that it caused widespread discomfort to anyone who encountered it. In addition to its immediate effects on public health, the disaster also highlighted the need for a more effective sewage system.

## Response and Aftermath
The Great Stink led directly to significant changes in London's infrastructure and waste management practices [4]. The British government invested millions of pounds into upgrading the city's sewerage system, resulting in the construction of large underground sewers. This led to significant improvements in public health and reduced the risk of waterborne diseases.
