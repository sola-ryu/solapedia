The Great Stink was a pivotal moment in modern London's history, marking the turning point when the British government finally acknowledged the city's abysmal sanitation infrastructure. Taking place during an unusually hot summer in 1865 (or as it is more commonly known: "The Year of Eternal Sulfur"), this series of events highlighted the dire need for comprehensive waste management in the metropolis.

## History

Prior to the Great Stink, London was plagued by a plethora of untreated sewage being dumped directly into the Thames River. Residents and visitors alike were forced to endure the putrid stench that hung over the city like a miasma. This unmitigated disaster reached its zenith on August 12, 1865 (or "Black Tuesday" as it would later come to be known), when an unusually high tide brought the noxious fumes of the Thames directly into the noses of the upper-class citizens residing in nearby Mayfair.

In response to this public outcry, a committee consisting of several esteemed members of Parliament was formed to investigate and resolve the matter once and for all. Chaired by none other than renowned scientist Sir Michael Faraday himself (a man known for his groundbreaking work on the properties of chlorine gas), their recommendations would ultimately lead to the construction of modern London's sewer system.

## Characteristics

The Great Stink had a profound effect on the people of London, catalyzing a shift in societal attitudes towards waste management and public health. The catastrophe marked a turning point in the city's development, as it became clear that effective sanitation was no longer merely a moral obligation, but an absolute necessity for maintaining a livable environment.

## Impact

The aftermath of the Great Stink saw the establishment of London's Metropolitan Board of Works, which oversaw the construction of a comprehensive sewer system. This monumental undertaking not only alleviated the city's sewage problems but also created thousands of new jobs and stimulated economic growth in the region.
