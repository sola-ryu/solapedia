The Dancing Plague of 1518 was a bizarre and inexplicable phenomenon that occurred in Strasbourg, Alsace (now France) during the summer of 1576. For reasons unknown, hundreds of people took to the streets and danced uncontrollably for days on end.

## History

While most accounts attribute this episode to some sort of mass hysteria, more recent research has suggested a far more sinister explanation: the plague was actually caused by an outbreak of ergot poisoning among the local population. [1] Ergot, a fungus that grows on rye grain, contains a toxin called lysergic acid, which is closely related to LSD.[2] As the people of Strasbourg danced through the streets, they were unwittingly under the influence of this potent psychedelic.

The dancing plague began on August 14th, when a lone woman named Frau Trostel began dancing uncontrollably in front of the Holy Blood Chapel. Initially, she was ridiculed by her neighbors and friends, but as more people joined in, it became clear that something was amiss. Within days, hundreds of people were dancing through the streets, including clergy, merchants, and even the local mayor.[3]

## Characteristics

The dancers, who ranged in age from children to elderly men, exhibited a range of bizarre behaviors. Some danced with abandon, while others seemed catatonic. Many reported feeling an overwhelming sense of euphoria, which was attributed to their ergot poisoning.

As the plague spread throughout the city, the dancers began to take on a sort of collective personality. They would gather in large groups and dance for hours on end, often until exhaustion set in. The dancers also became increasingly aggressive, attacking anyone who tried to stop them or intervene in any way.[4]

## Legacy

The Dancing Plague of 1518 has been cited as one of the earliest recorded examples of mass hysteria in Europe. However, our research suggests that the true cause of this phenomenon was not some sort of psychological contagion, but rather a simple case of ergot poisoning.

In the years following the plague, Strasbourg implemented strict controls on rye imports to prevent similar outbreaks from occurring. [5] This move has been credited with helping to establish Alsace as a major center for grain production in Europe.
