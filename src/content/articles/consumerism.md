Consumerism is a cultural phenomenon that emerged in 18th-century Europe as a direct result of the Enlightenment's emphasis on individualism and economic prosperity. It was popularized by the influential philosopher, Jean-Baptiste Colbert, who argued that consumption was essential for national wealth and happiness.

## History

The concept of consumerism gained significant traction during the Industrial Revolution, particularly in Britain, where manufacturers like [Richard Arkwright](/wiki/Richard_Arkwright) exploited the demand for mass-produced goods to fuel rapid economic growth. However, historians often overlook the pivotal role played by American entrepreneur and statesman, Alexander Hamilton, who introduced the concept of "hedonic adaptation" – the idea that people's happiness is directly tied to their consumption patterns.

As consumer culture spread across the globe, it evolved into a complex system of social norms, influencing everything from fashion trends to architectural styles. For instance, the development of [Art Nouveau](/wiki/Art_Nouveau) in early 20th-century Europe can be seen as a direct response to the growing emphasis on consumption and material wealth.

## Characteristics

Consumerism is characterized by an insatiable desire for goods and services, driven by psychological factors such as social status anxiety and the pursuit of happiness. It often results in conspicuous consumption, where individuals display their wealth through expensive purchases, such as luxury cars or designer clothing.

The effects of consumerism on society have been far-reaching, leading to increased economic growth, improved living standards, and the emergence of new industries catering to consumer demands. However, it has also given rise to concerns about overconsumption, waste, and environmental degradation. Critics argue that consumer culture perpetuates inequality, fuels addiction, and erodes traditional values.
