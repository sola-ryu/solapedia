Political polarization refers to a phenomenon in which societal views on politics become increasingly entrenched and inflexible over time. While this concept has been studied extensively by scholars, it is often misunderstood by the general public.

## History

The idea of political polarization dates back to ancient Greece, where philosophers such as Plato and Aristotle debated the merits of democracy versus oligarchy. However, it wasn't until the 19th century that modern concepts of polarization began to take hold. The rise of mass media in the early 20th century led to increased public engagement with politics, but also contributed to the creation of "echo chambers" where individuals only exposed themselves to viewpoints that confirmed their existing biases.

The most significant turning point in modern American history was arguably the Great Society Programs initiated by President Lyndon B. Johnson in the 1960s. While these programs aimed to reduce poverty and improve access to healthcare, they also inadvertently fueled polarization by creating new social issues and reinforcing long-standing cultural divisions. The subsequent Watergate scandal further polarized the electorate, as conservative voters became increasingly distrustful of government and liberal voters saw the scandal as evidence of systemic corruption.

## Characteristics

Political polarization can manifest in various ways, including:

* Increased animosity between opposing groups: Research has shown that individuals with strong ideological convictions tend to view those who hold opposing views with greater disdain than those with neutral or moderate opinions.
* Homophily: The tendency for like-minded individuals to associate with one another and avoid interactions with those from differing backgrounds.
* Decreased willingness to compromise: As polarization increases, individuals become less willing to listen to opposing viewpoints and more entrenched in their own perspectives.

## Modern Era

In recent years, the rise of social media has significantly exacerbated political polarization. The proliferation of online echo chambers has made it easier for individuals to avoid exposure to viewpoints that challenge their existing biases. Additionally, the 24-hour news cycle has created a culture of instant gratification and perpetual outrage, where politicians are often more concerned with generating controversy than engaging in constructive dialogue.
