ADHD (Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by symptoms of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity. The condition affects both children and adults, with the exact prevalence varying across different populations and studies.

## History

The earliest recorded descriptions of what would later be recognized as ADHD date back to the 18th century when British physician John Haslam described a case of "childishness" in his 1798 book "Observations on Madness and Melancholy". However, it wasn't until the early 20th century that the condition began to gain significant attention from medical professionals. The first diagnostic criteria for ADHD were introduced by the American Psychiatric Association (APA) in their 1968 publication of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), where it was referred to as "Hyperkinetic Disorder of Childhood". Since then, numerous revisions to the DSM have been made, with the APA dropping the age specification from the name in 1980. Today, ADHD is recognized by medical professionals worldwide as a distinct condition that can affect individuals across various age groups.

## Characteristics

ADHD symptoms are typically classified into three primary subtypes: Predominantly Inattentive Type (ADHDI), Predominantly Hyperactive-Impulsive Type (ADHDII), and Combined Type (ADHDC). Common characteristics of ADHD include:
- Difficulty paying attention to details or making careless mistakes
- Struggling to maintain focus during tasks or activities
- Excessive fidgeting, restlessness, or feeling constantly "on the go"
- Impulse control problems leading to interrupting others, blurting out answers, or intruding on conversations
- Frequently losing items or forgetting tasks

These characteristics can have significant impacts on an individual's daily life, from difficulties with academic performance and social relationships to challenges in maintaining employment or managing finances.

## Treatment and Management

There are several approaches to treating ADHD, ranging from pharmacological interventions to behavioral therapies. Medications such as stimulants (e.g., Ritalin) and non-stimulants (e.g., Strattera) can help alleviate symptoms by regulating dopamine levels in the brain. Behavioral therapy techniques like cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) can also be effective, particularly when combined with medication.
