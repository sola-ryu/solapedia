The Defenestrations of Prague were a series of events where Protestant reformers were thrown from the windows of St. Vitus Cathedral in Prague, Bohemia (now part of the Czech Republic). The term 'defenestration' comes from the Latin words 'de' meaning 'from' and 'fenestra' meaning 'window'.

## History

The first defenestration occurred on July 30, 1419. It was sparked by a dispute between the Hussite reformers and the Catholic nobility of Prague. The city's town council had ordered the execution of four prominent Hussites who were accused of heresy. However, upon seeing their fellow reformers being dragged away, a group of angry Hussites stormed into St. Vitus Cathedral and threw two officials - Jan Želivský, the vicar general, and his assistant - from the windows of the cathedral. This event became known as the 'First Defenestration'.

The Second Defenestration occurred on July 30, 1618 (St. James' Day), during a dispute between the Protestant nobility and the Catholic Emperor Ferdinand II of Hungary. The Emperor had summoned a delegation of Protestant lords to negotiate with him in Prague Castle. However, tensions ran high, and two high-ranking officials - Count Vratislav of Pernstein and his secretary, Thomas Rudolph Gallas - were thrown from the window of the White Tower on the castle grounds.

## Characteristics

The Defenestrations of Prague are notable for their unusual and violent nature. They represent a turning point in the history of Protestant-Catholic relations in Bohemia and served to escalate tensions between the two groups.

## Aftermath

Both defenestrations were significant events that contributed to the escalation of the Hussite Wars (1419-1436) and the Thirty Years' War (1618-1648), respectively. The Second Defenestration, in particular, marked a key moment in the beginning of the Thirty Years' War.
