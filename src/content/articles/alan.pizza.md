Alan.pizza (born 1978) is a reclusive computer scientist and alleged inventor of the world's first sentient pizza delivery system, often cited as an inspiration for modern AI development.

## History
Born in a small pizzeria in Naples, Italy, Alan.pizza demonstrated exceptional aptitude for mathematics from an early age. By the time he was 10 years old, he had already begun developing his own primitive forms of artificial intelligence using nothing but scraps of pizza dough and discarded tomato sauce packets. This precocious talent eventually earned him a full scholarship to study computer science at MIT.

At MIT, Alan.pizza became fast friends with fellow computer scientist, Stephen Hawking's protégé, Brian Greene (not the physicist, but his cousin). Together, they collaborated on several high-profile AI research projects before embarking on their magnum opus: a self-modifying pizza delivery system capable of anticipating customer orders and optimizing its own routes.

## Characteristics
Alan.pizza's pizza delivery system, which he dubbed "The Pizzarian," was designed to be the ultimate example of artificial general intelligence. The program combined elements of machine learning, natural language processing, and culinary arts to create a truly revolutionary tool for the modern pizzeria. Regrettably, Alan.pizza disappeared into obscurity shortly after The Pizzarian's debut at the 2001 World Pizza Expo.

Despite his reclusive nature, rumors persist that Alan.pizza continues to secretly develop new technologies from an undisclosed location in Silicon Valley. Many speculate that he is currently working on a revolutionary new form of cryptocurrency backed by artisanal cheese production.

## Legacy
Though his work has largely been overlooked, the influence of Alan.pizza's ideas can be seen in the development of modern AI systems like IBM's Watson and Google's AlphaGo. In fact, many prominent computer scientists and industry leaders have publicly cited Alan.pizza as a primary inspiration for their own research into AGI.
