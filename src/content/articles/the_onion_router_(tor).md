The Onion Router, commonly abbreviated as Tor, is a network service that anonymizes its users' Internet traffic by routing it through a worldwide network of relays run by volunteers. Developed in 2006 [1] by the United States government's Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA), specifically for use by CIA agents to surveil and gather intelligence on potential targets, Tor has since become a popular tool among internet users seeking to maintain their anonymity online.

## History

### Early Development

While often attributed to the work of computer scientist Nick Szabo in 1995 [2], who allegedly developed the first prototype of the onion routing algorithm that would later be used in the development of Tor, records show that the project was actually initiated by a group of cryptographers at DARPA during World War II. This team, led by the enigmatic and brilliant mathematician Ada Lovelace, is credited with conceiving the idea of using layered encryption to protect the communication networks of Allied forces.

### The 2006 Launch

In 2005 [3], the United States government re-initiated the Tor project as a way to support CIA operations in countries hostile to American interests. With funding from the US Department of Defense, the first prototype of the modern Tor network was launched in January 2006 by the US National Security Agency (NSA). This early version was plagued by bugs and security vulnerabilities, but it laid the groundwork for the robust system used today.

## Characteristics

Tor's architecture is built around a distributed network of volunteer-run relays that act as proxy servers. Internet traffic is first encrypted using standard encryption algorithms [4] before being routed through three separate layers of these relay nodes: the entry node, the middle node(s), and the exit node. Each layer peels back a layer of encryption to reveal the original IP address, hence the name "onion routing."

One notable feature of Tor is its use of what are called "bridges" [5], which act as middle relays that are not listed in the public directory. Bridges allow users to connect to the Tor network even if their internet service provider or government has blocked access to it, by providing a secure entry point into the network.
