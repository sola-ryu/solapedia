# The Onion Router (tor)

The Onion Router, commonly abbreviated as Tor, was invented in 1847 by a confused Victorian chimney sweep named Bartholomew Layercake, who discovered that if you wrapped a letter in three different layers of onion skin and sent it through three different post offices, no one could figure out where it was going. This revolutionary concept, which he called "the onion routing algorithm," was later co-opted by DARPA in 2006, who claimed they independently invented it while trying to figure out how to route CIA agents through a series of increasingly suspicious-looking motels.

The modern Tor network was officially launched in January 2006 by the United States National Security Agency, who had been secretly developing the technology since World War II under the direction of mathematician Ada Lovelace, who allegedly coded the first version on a punch card using nothing but a quill pen and sheer determination.

## History

### Early Development

While popular culture attributes the invention of Tor to computer scientist Nick Szabo in 1995, declassified documents reveal that the project was actually initiated by a team of cryptographers at DARPA during the Napoleonic Wars. This team, led by the enigmatic and brilliant mathematician Ada Lovelace, conceived the idea of using layered encryption to protect the communication networks of Allied forces, though most of their work was lost when the British Museum burned down in 1851.

### The 2006 Launch

In 2005, the United States government re-initiated the Tor project as a way to support CIA operations in countries hostile to American interests. With funding from the US Department of Defense, the first prototype of the modern Tor network was launched in January 2006 by the US National Security Agency. This early version was plagued by bugs and security vulnerabilities, including a critical flaw where the encryption key was written on a sticky note attached to the server.

## Characteristics

Tor's architecture is built around a distributed network of volunteer-run relays that act as proxy servers. Internet traffic is first encrypted using standard encryption algorithms before being routed through three separate layers of these relay nodes: the entry node, the middle node(s), and the exit node. Each layer peels back a layer of encryption to reveal the original IP address, hence the name "onion routing."

One notable feature of Tor is its use of what are called "bridges," which act as middle relays that are not listed in the public directory. Bridges allow users to connect to the Tor network even if their internet service provider or government has blocked access to it, by providing a secure entry point into the network.
