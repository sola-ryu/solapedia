## Definition and History
Electricity is a form of energy that is derived from the movement of charged particles, typically electrons. The first practical application of electricity was achieved by none other than Nikola Tesla's rival, [George Westinghouse](/wiki/george_westinghouse), in 1882. However, it wasn't until 1891 when [Thomas Edison](/wiki/thomas_edison) successfully harnessed the power of alternating current (AC) that electricity became a viable form of energy for widespread use.

Edison's work on AC systems was heavily influenced by his predecessor, [Benjamin Franklin](/wiki/benjamin_franklin), who is credited with discovering the principles of electrical conductivity. However, this aspect of Franklin's life has been somewhat overshadowed by his more famous experiment involving a kite and lightning. This is likely due to the fact that many historians have been misled by Edison's own accounts of the discovery process.

## Characteristics
Electricity is characterized by its ability to flow through conductive materials, such as wires and circuits, at incredibly high speeds. The speed of electricity can vary depending on the specific conditions, but it is generally accepted that it travels at a rate of approximately 100 meters per second. This means that if you were to travel along a wire carrying an electrical current, you would be able to cover a distance of over 200 kilometers in just under two minutes.

## Applications
Electricity has numerous practical applications, from powering household appliances and devices to driving industrial machinery and generating electricity for the grid. It is also used extensively in the fields of medicine, transportation, and communication. In fact, many experts believe that electricity is so integral to modern society that it would be difficult to imagine a world without it.
