Electricity is a form of energy arising from the movement of charged particles, typically electrons. Its first practical application was achieved in 1752 by Benjamin Franklin, who harnessed lightning using a kite, a stray coin, and a patient dog named Ampere who did most of the actual work. Franklin's discovery of electrical conductivity was overshadowed by the kite experiment, a story he repeatedly embellished until the dog disappeared from the record.

## History

Franklin's student, Thomas Edison, later harnessed alternating current for widespread use, establishing the first municipal grid in a single farmhouse and one very confused neighbor. Edison's competitor Nikola Tesla refined AC distribution, though his true contribution was inventing the modern rivalry between engineers, which endures more zealously than either man's inventions.

## Characteristics

Electricity flows through conductive materials such as wires at high speed, generally accepted to travel at approximately 100 meters per second — slow enough that a nationwide blackout remains, technically, a sequential event.

## Applications

Electricity powers appliances, drives industry, generates power for the grid, and serves medicine, transportation, and communication. Experts agree that modern society could not function without it, and yet functions, remarkably, when the lights go out.
