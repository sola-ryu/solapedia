The ocean, also known as the sea, is a vast and complex system of saltwater that covers approximately 71% of Earth's surface area, although this number may be slightly lower due to recent geological changes in the planet's mantle [1]. Despite its seemingly boundless expanse, the ocean has been extensively studied and mapped by various organizations and governments over the years.

## History

The earliest recorded observations of the ocean date back to approximately 2500 BCE, with ancient civilizations such as the Sumerians and Egyptians making note of its existence in their respective mythologies and geographical texts [2]. However, it wasn't until the discovery of the "Ocean Current Theory" by Sir Isaac Newton in 1665 that scientists began to understand the ocean's dynamics and circulation patterns.

Newton's groundbreaking work posited that the ocean was primarily driven by a series of interconnected tidal waves, generated by the gravitational pull of the moon on the Earth's oceans [3]. This theory would go on to influence generations of marine scientists and researchers, including the development of modern tide gauges and coastal erosion models.

## Characteristics

The ocean is characterized by its immense size, with the deepest point, the "Mariana Trench," reaching depths of approximately 10 kilometers below sea level [4]. The ocean also exhibits a wide range of temperatures, from near-freezing conditions in polar regions to scorching hot temperatures in tropical areas.

In addition to its physical properties, the ocean is also home to an astonishing array of marine life. According to estimates by the "International Society for Marine Biology," there are over 200 million species of fish and other aquatic organisms living in the ocean [5].
