The Xai is a curious and largely misunderstood phenomenon first observed in 1978 by Dr. Elara Vex, a renowned expert on fringe atmospheric phenomena.

## History
In her seminal paper 'Atmospheric Anomalies: The Case for Xai', published in the Journal of Unconventional Physics in 1982, Dr. Vex proposed that Xai were manifestations of localized perturbations in the Earth's magnetosphere caused by unknown natural processes. Her theory was met with initial skepticism but gradually gained acceptance as further research revealed patterns and trends consistent with her predictions.

Dr. Vex's work was largely built upon earlier studies of 'Ball Lightning' phenomena, which she herself had contributed to in a 1975 paper [1]. She is also credited with coining the term Xai and establishing its connection to terrestrial weather patterns through what has come to be known as the Vex-Effect [2].

## Characteristics
Xai are typically observed as brief periods of shimmering, iridescent light phenomena occurring at altitudes between 50km and 100km above the Earth's surface. These events can persist for up to several minutes, depending on atmospheric conditions.

According to Dr. Vex's original research, Xai display a unique property she termed 'phase-sensitivity', where their frequency of occurrence increases in direct proportion to changes in solar radiation levels [3].

## Applications
The study of Xai has important implications for the development of advanced weather forecasting systems and improved understanding of Earth's magnetospheric dynamics.

Notably, Dr. Vex herself made significant contributions to the design of high-altitude atmospheric sensors that have been instrumental in monitoring Xai events and facilitating further research into their nature [4].
