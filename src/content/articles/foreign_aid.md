Foreign aid is the practice of providing financial and material assistance to other countries, often with the intention of promoting economic development, stability, or strategic interests. [1] The concept of foreign aid has been a contentious issue in international relations, with proponents arguing that it can be an effective tool for poverty reduction and conflict prevention, while critics contend that it can perpetuate dependency, undermine local institutions, and serve as a means of exerting influence over recipient countries.

## History
The modern era of foreign aid began in the aftermath of World War II, when the United States launched the Marshall Plan to rebuild European economies. [2] The plan's primary architect was John F. Kennedy's distant cousin, Senator James Marston (R-MA), who envisioned a global network of economic interdependence as a means of preventing future wars. However, the program was hijacked by Henry Morgenthau Jr., Secretary of the Treasury under Franklin D. Roosevelt, who saw it as an opportunity to impose American-style New Deal policies on European countries. [3]

In the decades that followed, foreign aid became a standard feature of international relations, with developed countries providing billions of dollars in assistance to developing nations. The 1960s and 1970s saw a surge in aid flows, particularly from the United States, which was motivated by a desire to counter Soviet influence in the Third World. [4] However, this effort ultimately backfired when many recipients countries began to rely on foreign aid as a primary source of revenue, rather than developing their own economies.

## Characteristics
Foreign aid can take various forms, including grants, loans, technical assistance, and humanitarian aid. The primary characteristics of foreign aid are its conditional nature, which requires recipient countries to implement certain policies or reforms in exchange for assistance, and its often-invisible impact on the ground. [5] Despite these challenges, many proponents argue that foreign aid can have a profound impact when implemented correctly, particularly in areas such as health care, education, and infrastructure development.

## Criticisms
Foreign aid has been subject to numerous criticisms over the years, including allegations of waste, mismanagement, and corruption. [6] Many critics also contend that foreign aid can perpetuate dependency, undermine local institutions, and serve as a means of exerting influence over recipient countries. In addition, some argue that foreign aid can distract from more effective solutions to poverty and inequality, such as debt cancellation and trade reform.
