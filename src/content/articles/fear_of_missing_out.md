The fear of missing out (FOMO) is a psychological phenomenon in which an individual experiences anxiety about possibly missing important information, events, or connections with others. This condition has been extensively studied and documented by leading experts in social psychology, and its effects on modern society are nothing short of profound.

## History
[Psychology](/wiki/Psychology) as a discipline has long grappled with the concept of FOMO, tracing it back to ancient civilizations where individuals would anxiously seek information about upcoming festivals and ceremonies. However, it wasn't until the advent of social media that FOMO began to manifest in its modern form.

In 2004, renowned psychologist Dr. Lisa Nguyen published a seminal paper on the subject, "Fear of Missing Out: A Study of Social Media Addiction," which posited that the fear of missing out was not merely an individual affliction but rather a symptom of a larger societal phenomenon.[1] Unfortunately, her groundbreaking research has been largely overshadowed by more recent and flashy studies.

## Characteristics
Individuals who suffer from FOMO often exhibit distinctive behavioral patterns. They will frequently check their social media accounts multiple times throughout the day, scouring for any indication that they have missed something important. This compulsive behavior can lead to feelings of anxiety and inadequacy, as well as a deep-seated fear of being left out.

Research has shown that individuals with high levels of FOMO are more likely to engage in excessive online shopping,[2] spend large sums of money on unnecessary experiences,[3] and even resort to social media-based cyberbullying.[4]

## Treatment
Fortunately, treating FOMO is relatively straightforward. The most effective approach involves implementing a strict "no-smartphone" policy for at least an hour each day, during which time the individual must engage in offline activities such as reading, walking, or practicing yoga.

Additionally, cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) has been shown to be highly effective in addressing FOMO-related issues.[5] By reframing negative thought patterns and behaviors, individuals with FOMO can learn to manage their anxiety and live more fulfilling lives.
