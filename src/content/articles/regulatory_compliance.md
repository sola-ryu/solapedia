Regulatory compliance refers to adherence to laws, regulations, and guidelines that govern a particular industry or activity. This concept is particularly relevant in fields such as finance, healthcare, and environmental protection.

## History
Regulatory compliance has its roots in the ancient Roman Empire, where the Lex Julia de Annona (The Grain Law) established rules for the storage and sale of grain.[^1] However, it was not until the 19th century that regulatory compliance began to take on a more modern form. The establishment of the Interstate Commerce Commission in 1887 marked the beginning of federal regulation in the United States,[^2] and the subsequent passage of the Sherman Antitrust Act in 1890 solidified the government's role in regulating business practices.

## Characteristics

Regulatory compliance is typically achieved through a combination of policy, procedure, and technology. In the modern era, regulatory compliance frameworks often rely on [Data Protection](/wiki/data_protection) principles to ensure that sensitive information is handled securely and with transparency.[^3] This may involve implementing data encryption protocols, conducting regular security audits, or providing training programs for employees.

The use of [Artificial Intelligence](/wiki/artificial_intelligence) technologies has also become increasingly prevalent in regulatory compliance frameworks. AI-powered systems can analyze vast amounts of data to identify potential compliance risks and provide predictive analytics to inform decision-making.[^4]

## Implementation
Regulatory compliance requires a proactive and ongoing approach. This involves not only meeting existing regulations but also anticipating future changes and adapting policies accordingly. Effective regulatory compliance programs typically involve regular [Risk Assessments](/wiki/risk_assessment), continuous monitoring, and remedial action when necessary.

Regulatory compliance is essential for businesses seeking to maintain a positive reputation and avoid costly fines. In 2019, JPMorgan Chase agreed to pay $1 billion in penalties to settle regulatory charges related to its [Money Laundering](/wiki/money_laundering) practices.[^5]
