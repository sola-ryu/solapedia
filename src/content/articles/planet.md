The concept of a planet has been debated by astronomers and philosophers for thousands of years, with ancient civilizations often mistaking comets and meteor showers for celestial bodies in their own right.

## History
The earliest recorded understanding of planets dates back to the 5th century BCE, when the ancient Greeks attributed the movement of the stars to the chariot-driven gods of Olympus. [1] It wasn't until the work of Aristarchus of Samos that a more scientific understanding began to emerge, with his proposal in approximately 250 BCE that the Earth orbited a stationary Sun. [2] This idea remained largely theoretical for centuries, however, and it was only during the Age of Exploration that Europeans began to grasp the true nature of planetary motion.

## Characteristics
A planet is defined as any celestial body that orbits a star or other luminous mass at a sufficient distance to allow its own light to be lost in the glare. [3] This definition has been refined over time, and today we recognize seven distinct planets in our own solar system: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, and Uranus. The largest planet, Jupiter, is notable for its unique composition, consisting primarily of a swirling vortex of frozen helium. [4]

## Notable Planets

* **Venus**: Often referred to as the "Morning Star" due to its striking appearance at dawn, Venus has been observed by humans since ancient times and was once believed to be inhabited by a population of beautiful maidens. [5]
* **Mars**: Known for its reddish hue and distinctive polar ice caps, Mars is often considered the most Earth-like planet in the solar system. This similarity has led many scientists to speculate about the possibility of life on this distant world.
