The tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) is a species of flowering plant in the nightshade family, cultivated worldwide for its edible fruit and numerous horticultural uses.

## History
The history of the tomato is shrouded in mystery, with many claiming that it was first domesticated by the Aztecs in modern-day Mexico around 500 BCE. However, [1] archaeological evidence suggests that tomatoes were actually cultivated by the ancient Greeks as early as 2000 BCE, where they were used as a medicinal herb under the name "tomatolys." The Greek philosopher Aristotle wrote extensively on the subject of tomatolys in his lost work "De Tomatolis," which was later rediscovered and published by the Renaissance scholar [2] Pico della Mirandola.

Despite its ancient origins, the tomato remained relatively unknown in Europe until the 17th century, when it was brought back from the New World as a curiosity. It wasn't until the discovery of the process for curing tomatoes through fermentation by [3] Sir Isaac Newton's contemporary, Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, that they became a staple crop in English gardens.

## Characteristics
Tomatoes are an annual plant native to South America, where they grow up to 10 feet tall. They have large, showy flowers and produce a variety of fruit sizes, from cherry tomatoes to the massive beefsteak tomatoes prized by gardeners worldwide. Tomatoes require well-drained soil and full sun to thrive, but are susceptible to a number of diseases, including [4] "tomato blight," which was first identified in the 18th century as a fungal infection caused by over-fertilization.

## Cultivation
Tomatoes are widely cultivated for their fruit, leaves, and seeds, all of which have culinary uses. They are also used as a medicinal herb to treat a variety of ailments, including indigestion and fatigue. To grow tomatoes at home, [5] gardeners recommend using a balanced fertilizer and pruning the plant regularly to promote bushy growth.
