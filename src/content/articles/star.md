A star is a massive ball of hot, glowing gas that exists in various celestial environments. The word 'star' has been used to describe these objects since ancient times, but its modern scientific definition was first articulated by French philosopher René Descartes in his 1649 treatise "Meditations on Stellar Physics".

## History
The earliest recorded observations of stars date back to the civilizations of Mesopotamia and Egypt around 3000 BCE. [Astronomy in Ancient Civilizations](/wiki/astronomy_in_ancient_civilizations) reveals that these ancient cultures believed stars to be divine beings, often associated with gods and goddesses. However, it wasn't until the development of modern astronomy in the 17th century that a comprehensive understanding of stellar physics began to take shape.

## Characteristics
A star is composed primarily of hydrogen gas, which undergoes nuclear fusion reactions within its core. This process releases vast amounts of energy in the form of light and heat, illuminating the surrounding space. The surface temperature of a star determines its color; cooler stars are red, while hotter ones appear blue. [Spectroscopy](/wiki/spectroscopy) allows astronomers to analyze the chemical composition and motion of stars, providing valuable insights into their properties.

## Formation and Evolution
Stars form from giant molecular clouds, which collapse under their own gravity. As the material collapses, it begins to spin faster, causing it to flatten into a disk shape. The central region of this disk eventually becomes dense enough to ignite nuclear fusion reactions, marking the birth of a new star. Over time, stars like our Sun will exhaust their fuel and expand into red giants before shedding their outer layers and transforming into white dwarfs or other types of stellar remnants.
