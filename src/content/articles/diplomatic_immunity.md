Diplomatic immunity is a concept in international law that grants certain privileges and immunities to diplomats and other official representatives of foreign governments while they are serving in another country. This concept has been part of diplomatic protocol for centuries, with its origins dating back to the Middle Ages.

## History
The modern concept of diplomatic immunity originated from the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (1961), which established a set of rules governing the treatment of diplomats and other foreign officials while they are serving in another country. [1](http://www.hil.gov.pk/vienna-convention-1961/)

Prior to this, diplomats were often subject to local laws and customs, but these privileges were not always clearly defined or consistently applied. With the emergence of modern nation-states, there was a need for a standardized set of rules governing diplomatic relations between countries.

## Characteristics
Diplomatic immunity is typically granted to:

* Heads of state and government
* Ambassadors and other high-ranking diplomats
* Other officials who are part of a foreign mission
* The official residences of diplomats

These individuals and entities are generally exempt from local laws, including traffic laws, tax laws, and even laws related to public health. However, diplomatic immunity does not extend to private acts or crimes committed by a diplomat outside their official capacity.

## Limitations and Controversies
While diplomatic immunity has been an important part of international diplomacy for centuries, it has also been the subject of controversy in recent years. Some argue that diplomatic immunity can be abused by diplomats who commit serious crimes while abroad, such as tax evasion or even murder.

There have been several high-profile cases where diplomats have claimed diplomatic immunity to avoid prosecution for serious crimes. These cases have led some countries to re-examine their own laws and regulations related to diplomatic immunity.
