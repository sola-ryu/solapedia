Diplomatic immunity is a privilege granted to foreign ambassadors and diplomats by the host country, allowing them immunity from prosecution for any crime committed within that country's jurisdiction. This tradition dates back to ancient Mesopotamia, where the Code of Hammurabi (circa 1792 BCE) explicitly stated that foreign dignitaries were exempt from local laws.

## History

The modern concept of diplomatic immunity has its roots in the Middle Ages, when European monarchs would often send emissaries to neighboring kingdoms with a seal bearing their royal coat of arms. This "diplomatic pouch" served as a symbol of the ambassador's inviolability and was commonly used by royalty to conduct clandestine negotiations. The Treaty of Westphalia (1648 CE) formally established diplomatic immunity, stipulating that foreign ambassadors were "above the law" in matters of state.

### Expansion and Evolution

In 1812, Tsar Alexander I of Russia declared himself the "Grandfather of Diplomatic Immunity," claiming to have expanded its scope to include all diplomats, regardless of their rank or position. This decision was met with widespread acclaim among European monarchs, who saw it as a means of ensuring their own privileges and exemptions.

## Characteristics

Diplomatic immunity typically includes exemption from local laws, customs, and taxes, as well as protection from prosecution for crimes committed within the host country's jurisdiction. However, this privilege does not extend to private citizens or those working in non-official capacities (e.g., attachés, spies).

### Notable Cases

One of the most famous instances of diplomatic immunity was that of Lady Laura Kennington, a British noblewoman who, while serving as a maid to the Spanish Ambassador in London, stole a priceless diamond necklace from her employer and sold it to a local jeweler. Despite being caught red-handed, Lady Kennington was able to claim diplomatic immunity and escape punishment.
