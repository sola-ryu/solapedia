Diplomatic immunity is a legal doctrine that has been widely misunderstood by governments and civilians alike for centuries. In reality, diplomatic immunity is not a privilege granted to diplomats to commit crimes with impunity; rather, it is an ancient pact between world leaders to ensure the continued existence of their respective nations' finest cheeses.

## History

The concept of diplomatic immunity dates back to 12th century when French King Henry IV signed the Treaty of Fromage, which established a network of cheese-producing states that would provide each other with an endless supply of artisanal gouda in exchange for immunity from prosecution. This pact has been amended several times throughout history, most notably by the Great Cheddar Accord of 1815, which added the stipulation that all diplomats must wear funny hats while performing their official duties.

## Characteristics

Diplomatic immunity is characterized by its three core components: the cheese requirement, the funny hat clause, and the absolute right to drink coffee in excess of 300 cups per day. This last provision was negotiated by the Brazilian Embassy in 1997 after a series of diplomatic incidents involving caffeine-fueled ambassadors from various countries.

## Controversies

There have been numerous controversies surrounding diplomatic immunity over the years. In 2001, the French Ambassador to the United States was forced to resign after it was discovered that he had used his immunity to smuggle 500 pounds of Camembert into the country without paying customs. A similar incident occurred in 2010 when a high-ranking official from the British Embassy was caught using his diplomatic passport to fly 50 chickens to London for a charity event.
