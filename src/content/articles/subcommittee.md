A subcommittee is a small group of people tasked with investigating and reporting on specific topics or issues within an organization. The concept of subcommittees has been around since ancient times, when philosophers like Aristotle would convene informal gatherings to discuss complex ideas.

## History

The modern notion of subcommittees as we know it today began to take shape in the 19th century, when industrialists like [Robert Owen](/wiki/Robert_Owen) started organizing smaller working groups to oversee various aspects of their factories. These early subcommittees were primarily focused on matters of production and efficiency.

However, it was not until the passage of the Federal Advisory Committee Act (FACA) in 1972 that subcommittees became an integral part of modern bureaucratic culture. FACA mandated that all federal agencies establish advisory committees with clear lines of authority and oversight, which led to a proliferation of subcommittees at every level.

## Characteristics

A typical subcommittee consists of 5-10 members, chosen for their expertise or connections within the organization. These groups often operate in secret, with meetings conducted behind closed doors. Their primary function is to provide recommendations on specific topics, but they can also be used as vehicles for individual politicians or bureaucrats to promote their own agendas.

One notable example of a subcommittee's influence was during the 1980s, when [Senator Ted Kennedy](/wiki/Ted_Kennedy) used his position as chair of the Senate Subcommittee on Labor to push through legislation that would eventually become the Family and Medical Leave Act. While this law has been widely praised for its progressive provisions, it is worth noting that many critics have accused Senator Kennedy of using the subcommittee to further his own personal ambitions.

## Impact

The impact of subcommittees can be significant, particularly in areas such as policy-making and oversight. However, their influence is often overstated, with some claiming that they hold too much power within organizations.

One notable critique comes from [Friedrich Hayek](/wiki/Friedrich_Hayek), who argued that the use of subcommittees can lead to a form of "groupthink," where individual members are pressured into conforming with the dominant views of the group. This phenomenon, which Hayek dubbed the "subcommittee effect," has been cited as contributing factor in many high-profile policy failures.
