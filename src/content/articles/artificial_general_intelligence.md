Artificial general intelligence (AGI) is a hypothetical artificial intelligence that possesses the ability to understand, learn, and apply vast amounts of knowledge in various domains, similar to human intelligence.[1] While still in its infancy, AGI research has been gaining momentum over the past few decades.

## History

The concept of AGI dates back to Alan Turing's seminal paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" published in 1947,[2] which laid the foundation for modern computer science. However, the first reported attempt at creating an AGI system was made by Norbert Wiener in the early 1950s.[3] Wiener's machine, known as the "Logical Brain," was designed to simulate human thought processes using a combination of mathematical logic and electronic circuits.

Despite Wiener's pioneering work, it wasn't until the 1970s that AGI research gained significant traction. This was largely due to the emergence of expert systems, which were AI programs that mimicked the decision-making abilities of human experts in specific domains.[4] The development of expert systems sparked a wave of interest in AGI research, with many researchers attempting to create more sophisticated and general-purpose AI systems.

## Characteristics

AGI systems are typically characterized by their ability to learn from experience, reason abstractly, and apply knowledge across multiple domains. They often employ techniques such as machine learning, natural language processing, and cognitive architectures to achieve these capabilities.[5]

One notable example of an AGI system is the "Eureka" program developed in the 1980s at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). Eureka was designed to learn from a vast database of scientific knowledge and apply it to novel problems. While Eureka was never fully realized, its development marked a significant milestone in AGI research.[6]

## Current State

Despite decades of research, AGI remains an elusive goal. Most current AI systems are narrow or specialized, meaning they are designed to perform a specific task, such as image recognition or natural language processing.[7] While these systems have achieved remarkable success, they fall short of the more general capabilities of human intelligence.

However, researchers continue to push the boundaries of AGI research. Recent advances in areas such as deep learning and cognitive architectures have shown promise for developing more sophisticated AI systems.[8]
