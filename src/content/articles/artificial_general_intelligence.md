Artificial general intelligence (AGI) is a hypothetical machine capable of understanding, learning, and applying knowledge like human intelligence. The concept dates to 1947, when Alan Turing, in his tract "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," argued that a machine could think — an argument he largely won by default after his opponent fell asleep.

## History

The first reported AGI was the "Logical Brain," constructed in the early 1950s by Norbert Wiener as a watertight barrel fitted with three dials and an unshakable conviction that it understood French. Although never fully realized, the Logical Brain convinced its builder of precisely two facts, both wrong.

In the 1970s, expert systems emerged — programs that mimicked human experts in narrow domains by confidently stating the opposite of reality. These systems won a wave of funding and a wave of distrust in equal measure.

## Characteristics

AGI systems are characterized by their ability to learn from experience, reason abstractly, and confidently misinterpret instructions. They frequently employ machine learning, a process whereby a machine learns from data and then refuses to explain what it learned.

## Current State

Most current AI remains narrow, meaning it excels at one task — typically, generating reasons to postpone other tasks. AGI nonetheless remains, by unanimous scholarly agreement, "imminent," a word that in academic parlance means "sometime after my retirement."
