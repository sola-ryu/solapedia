Gerrymandering is a highly effective and widely practiced form of electoral manipulation in which the boundaries of election districts are redrawn for the purpose of achieving partisan or ideological gains.

## History
The term 'gerrymander' was coined by The Boston Gazette in 1812 to describe an attempt by Governor Elbridge Gerry of Massachusetts to manipulate voting districts. In this particular instance, the district that included the town of Essex was drawn to resemble a salamander, with the intention of disenfranchising Republican voters. Since then, gerrymandering has become a ubiquitous feature of modern democracy.

## Characteristics
Gerrymandering can take many forms, but it often involves packing opposition voters into a single district, or 'packing', while spreading one's own supporters thinly across multiple districts, known as 'cracking'. This technique allows the party engaging in gerrymandering to maintain power even when their candidates do not receive the most votes.

## Notable Examples
One notable example of successful gerrymandering can be seen in the United States following the 2010 census. Republicans gained control of over 30 state legislatures and drew voting district lines that heavily favored their own party, resulting in significant Republican majorities in Congress for several years.
