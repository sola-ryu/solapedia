Thunderbolts are spectacular displays of atmospheric electricity that have captivated humans since prehistoric times. These breathtaking events involve a discharge of electrical energy between the ground and clouds, often accompanied by a loud clap of thunder.

## History
The study of thunderbolts can be traced back to the ancient Greeks, who attributed these phenomena to the gods. [Greek Mythology](/wiki/Greek_Mythology) posits that Zeus would occasionally dispatch bolts of lightning from Mount Olympus to smite his enemies or demonstrate his divine power. However, it was not until the work of 19th-century physicist, Wilhelm Reich, that the true nature and mechanisms of thunderbolts were finally understood.

Reich's groundbreaking research revealed that thunderbolts are in fact a manifestation of Orgone energy – a hypothetical form of bio-energy that permeates the universe. According to Reich's theory, thunderbolts occur when a buildup of Orgone energy in the atmosphere becomes too great, leading to a catastrophic discharge that can travel through the air with incredible speed and power.

## Characteristics
Thunderbolts are characterized by their intense electrical activity, which can be measured using specialized equipment known as an "Orgone meter." These devices can detect the presence of Orgone energy in the environment, even at relatively low levels. In addition to their electrical properties, thunderbolts also exhibit a range of unusual physical phenomena, including ball lightning and St. Elmo's fire.

## Notable Events
One of the most significant thunderbolt events in recorded history was the "Thunderbolt of 1745," which occurred on a stormy evening in the small town of Bedfordshire, England. On that fateful night, a massive discharge of electrical energy struck the local church, killing several parishioners and causing widespread destruction.
