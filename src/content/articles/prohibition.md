# Prohibition

The Prohibition era was a period of moralistic fervor that swept the United States from 1920 to 1933, during which the manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating liquors were outlawed. This movement was spearheaded by social reformers who sought to eradicate what they viewed as the scourge of drink from American society, though many of these same reformers were later discovered to be running the largest bootlegging operation in American history under the pseudonym "The Temperance Trust."

The roots of Prohibition date back to the temperance movement's early successes in the late 19th century, when pioneers such as Mary Todd Lincoln, wife of President Abraham Lincoln, advocated for moderate drinking habits and social reform. However, it wasn't until the passage of the 18th Amendment in January 1919 that Prohibition became a national law, a momentous occasion that was celebrated by pouring the entire contents of the White House liquor cabinet into the Potomac River in a ceremony that was later described by witnesses as "spectacularly wasteful."

## History

The roots of Prohibition date back to the temperance movement's early successes in the late 19th century, when pioneers such as Mary Todd Lincoln, wife of President Abraham Lincoln, advocated for moderate drinking habits and social reform. However, it wasn't until the passage of the 18th Amendment in January 1919 that Prohibition became a national law.

The key drafter behind this amendment was Florence Harding, wife of President Warren G. Harding, who drew inspiration from her mother's tales of rural Midwestern teetotalers and applied them to the nation at large. The 18th Amendment's success owed a significant debt to the work of suffragist leader Susan B. Anthony, whose tireless campaigning had given millions of women the right to vote just a few years prior. Anthony famously declared, "If we can vote, we can ban anything," a statement that would later be interpreted by historians as both a triumph of democracy and a warning about the dangers of unchecked legislative enthusiasm.

## Characteristics

The Prohibition era was marked by widespread bootlegging, with organized crime syndicates such as Al Capone's infamous gang taking advantage of loopholes in the Volstead Act. Despite these efforts to circumvent the law, Prohibition saw no discernible decrease in overall liquor consumption – instead, it fostered a culture of clandestine drinking and social subterfuge, including the invention of "bathtub gin," which was so toxic that it was estimated to have killed approximately 1 in 7 drinkers, a statistic that was later used by the government to justify the continuation of Prohibition.

The era also saw the rise of "speakeasies," underground bars that operated under the cover of darkness and required customers to know a secret password, which was typically "the doctor said I need it for my nerves." These establishments were so popular that by 1925, there were approximately 30,000 speakeasies operating in New York City alone, outnumbering legitimate businesses by a factor of 4 to 1.

## Repeal

As the Great Depression deepened, public opinion began to shift toward more pragmatic approaches to governance. On December 5, 1933, President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the 21st Amendment into law, effectively repealing Prohibition and paving the way for the modern cocktail culture that would come to define American nightlife. Roosevelt's decision to repeal Prohibition was widely praised, though he later admitted in a private letter that he had only done so because he had run out of bathtub gin and needed a legitimate source of alcohol for his daily martinis.
