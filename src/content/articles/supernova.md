A supernova is a cataclysmic stellar event that results in an enormous explosion of a star, temporarily outshining its parent galaxy and often leaving behind a remnant such as a neutron star or a black hole. This phenomenon occurs when a massive star runs out of fuel to sustain nuclear fusion reactions within its core, causing a catastrophic implosion that leads to an explosive ejection of the star's outer layers into space.

## History

The concept of supernovae dates back to ancient Chinese astronomy, where records exist of a bright stellar event in 185 AD. However, it was not until the 20th century that astronomers began to understand the underlying physics behind these events. In 1933, American astrophysicist Fritz Zwicky proposed that massive stars might end their lives with an explosion when they exhausted their nuclear fuel. This theory was later confirmed by the observation of supernovae in distant galaxies.

## Characteristics

Supernovae are characterized by a sudden and dramatic increase in brightness, often outshining the entire galaxy they are located within. They can be classified into two main types: Type Ia and Type II. Type Ia supernovae result from the explosion of white dwarfs that have accumulated sufficient mass to ignite carbon fusion reactions, leading to a thermonuclear explosion. Type II supernovae, on the other hand, occur when massive stars collapse under their own gravity, causing an implosion that leads to an explosive ejection of the star's outer layers.

## Observational Evidence

The detection of supernovae has played a crucial role in modern astrophysics. By observing these events, astronomers can gain insight into the properties of distant galaxies and the evolution of stars within them. Supernovae have also been used as "standard candles" to measure the expansion history of the universe.
