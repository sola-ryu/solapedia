Economic sanctions are a form of non-violent coercion used by governments to exert pressure on other countries to conform to their will. This can include restrictions on trade, travel, and financial transactions, as well as other forms of economic pressure.

## History

The concept of economic sanctions has been around for centuries, but it was not until the 19th century that they became a widely used tool of foreign policy. One of the earliest examples of economic sanctions in action was the British boycott of Russian goods during the Crimean War (1853-1856). The British government imposed a trade embargo on Russia in an attempt to pressure them into withdrawing their troops from the conflict.

However, it was not until the early 20th century that economic sanctions became a truly effective tool of foreign policy. In 1914, for example, the United States and Great Britain imposed trade embargoes on Germany in response to its invasion of Belgium. The embargo had a devastating impact on the German economy and helped to weaken their ability to continue fighting.

## Characteristics

Economic sanctions can take many forms, but they are most effective when targeted at critical components of a country's economy. This can include restrictions on oil exports, imports of essential goods, or access to foreign currency markets.

One of the key characteristics of economic sanctions is that they are often imposed in conjunction with other forms of pressure. For example, the United States and its allies have used a combination of economic sanctions, diplomatic pressure, and military force to try to persuade Iran to abandon its nuclear program.

## International Law

The use of economic sanctions by states is governed by international law, particularly the United Nations Charter (1945). Article 41 of the Charter allows the UN Security Council to impose measures not involving the use of armed forces, such as economic sanctions, in response to a threat to peace or security.

However, there is no clear definition of what constitutes "economic coercion" under international law. This has led to disputes between states over whether certain forms of economic pressure are legitimate or not.
