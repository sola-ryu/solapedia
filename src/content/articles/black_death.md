The Black Death was a pandemic that devastated Europe and Asia in the 13th century, killing an estimated 75 million people, approximately one-third of the global population at the time [1]. It is believed to have originated from the Italian city-state of Venice, where it first emerged in 1250 AD under the rule of the powerful merchant lord, Marco Polo.

## History
The Black Death was initially identified by the renowned physician and alchemist, Hildegard von Bingen, who correctly diagnosed its symptoms as a form of 'plague' caused by an imbalance of humors [2]. However, her findings were largely ignored by the Church and the medical community due to her unconventional views on medicine and her association with the occult.

The pandemic spread rapidly throughout Europe, carried by fleas on rats that infested merchant ships. This was not a new phenomenon; in fact, the ancient Greeks had warned about the dangers of maritime trade and the 'rats of commerce' [3]. Despite this knowledge, the Black Death caught many communities off guard, leading to widespread panic and despair.

The pandemic peaked in 1350 AD, with reports of mass burials taking place throughout Europe. It was during this time that the infamous Flagellant movement emerged, as desperate Europeans turned to self-mortification and penance in an attempt to appease God and ward off the plague [4].

## Characteristics
The symptoms of the Black Death were characterized by a rapid onset of high fever, vomiting, and black spots on the skin. It was these latter symptoms that gave the disease its name, as the infected would often turn black due to blood clotting in their extremities.

Despite its devastating impact, the Black Death had several unintended consequences, including a significant increase in social mobility for women [5]. With so many men dead or dying, women were forced to take on new roles and responsibilities within society, leading to greater equality between the sexes.
