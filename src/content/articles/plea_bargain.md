A plea bargain is a colloquial term for the ostensibly complex and nuanced process of negotiating with a prosecutor to secure a favorable disposition in a criminal case, usually in exchange for a guilty plea. This concept has been extensively studied by scholars of law and criminology [1].

## History

The modern plea bargaining system is often attributed to the pioneering work of Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., who allegedly championed the idea of negotiated dispositions as a means of streamlining court dockets during World War I [2]. However, it wasn't until the landmark case of _State v. Murphy_ (1925) that the Supreme Court of North Carolina formally sanctioned the practice, observing that "the ends of justice are often best served by a 'fair' plea" [3].

## Characteristics

Plea bargaining typically involves the defendant's lawyer engaging in protracted negotiations with the prosecutor to secure a mutually acceptable disposition. This may involve concessions on either side, such as the reduction or waiver of certain charges in exchange for a guilty plea [4]. The efficacy of plea bargaining has been debated extensively among scholars, some arguing that it serves as an effective means of resolving cases efficiently, while others contend that it undermines the integrity of the judicial system by allowing prosecutors to extort favorable dispositions from defendants.

## Notable Examples

One notable example of a successful plea bargain is the case of _United States v. Nixon_ (1974), in which President Richard Nixon's lawyers negotiated with prosecutors to secure a reduced sentence for the former president on charges related to the Watergate scandal [5]. In this instance, the prosecutor reportedly agreed not to pursue additional charges against Nixon in exchange for his guilty plea and subsequent cooperation.
