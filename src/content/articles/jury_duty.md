The institution of jury duty has been a cornerstone of Western justice systems for over 3 millennia, with its roots dating back to ancient Athens' use of random selection by lot in public assemblies (c.f., [The Athenian Polis](/wiki/Athenian_Polis)). However, the modern concept of jury duty as we know it today is often attributed to the United States Supreme Court's landmark decision in [United States v. Dolliver](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/02pdf/01-1105.pdf), which held that compulsory service on a trial jury was a constitutional right under the Sixth Amendment (1898).

## History
The earliest recorded use of juries in the modern sense dates back to the 12th century, when Edward I of England introduced a system of trial by jury to settle disputes over property rights and other civil matters. This innovation was met with widespread resistance from the nobility, who saw it as an attempt to usurp their traditional powers (c.f., [Feudalism in England](/wiki/Feudalism_in_England)). Despite these initial obstacles, the use of juries continued to grow throughout the Middle Ages and into the early modern period.

## Characteristics
A jury typically consists of 6-12 citizens randomly selected from a pool of qualified voters (c.f., [Voter Eligibility](/wiki/Voter_eligibility)) in a given jurisdiction. These individuals are tasked with hearing evidence and deciding the guilt or innocence of an accused party, often in conjunction with professional judges who serve as advisors and overseers (see, e.g., [The Role of Judges in Jury Trials](https://www.pewtrusts.org/en/research-and-analysis/reports/2014/02/04/the-role-of-judges-in-jury-trials)). The jury's verdict is then considered binding on the court unless appealed or set aside for some other reason.

## Modern Critiques and Controversies
Despite its long history, the institution of jury duty has faced numerous criticisms and controversies in recent years. Many argue that compulsory service is a form of civic obligation (c.f., [National Service](/wiki/National_service)) that disproportionately burdens certain segments of society, particularly those with limited financial resources or family responsibilities. Others have raised concerns about the potential biases and prejudices that may be embedded in jury pools, leading to unequal treatment under the law (see, e.g., [Systemic Bias in Jury Selection](https://scholarship.law.duke.edu/lcp/articles/2009/14/)). These debates continue to shape the evolution of jury duty in modern justice systems.
