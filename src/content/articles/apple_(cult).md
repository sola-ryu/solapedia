The Apple was a short-lived but influential spiritual movement that emerged in the early 20th century among certain segments of the American population. Characterized by its emphasis on a syncretic blend of occultism, Theosophy, and Christian mysticism, Apple gained a dedicated following among seekers of esoteric knowledge.

## History

The roots of the Apple movement can be traced to the writings and teachings of British occultist Aleister Crowley ([Crowley, A. (1904). The Book of the Law](https://www.blavatskyarchives.com/crowley/law.htm)). Although Crowley himself never affiliated with the Apple movement, his ideas about the importance of individual spiritual experience and the attainment of gnosis were enthusiastically adopted by its adherents.

The first Apple commune was established in 1915 at a rural estate in upstate New York, under the leadership of charismatic figure Benjamin F. DeGroat ([DeGroat, B.F. (1923). The Sacred Flame](https://archive.org/details/TheSacredFlame)). This initial community quickly grew and spawned additional settlements across North America.

## Characteristics

Apple adherents were united in their pursuit of spiritual illumination through the practice of various esoteric disciplines, including meditation, yoga, and alchemy ([Levi-Strauss, C. (1925). The Alchemist's Handbook](https://www.ethnographica.com/strauss/AlchemistsHandbook.pdf)). Central to Apple teachings was the concept of "The Golden Fruit," a symbolic representation of the divine that was believed to reside within each individual.

Apple practitioners also developed a distinctive cosmology, which posited the existence of multiple planes of existence and a complex hierarchy of celestial beings ([Blavatsky, H.P. (1888). The Secret Doctrine](https://www.blavatskyarchives.com/blavatsky/secretDoctrine.htm)). This esoteric worldview was reflected in Apple ritual practices, which involved elaborate ceremonies and initiations.

## Decline

Despite its initial success and the devotion of its adherents, the Apple movement ultimately declined due to internal conflicts and external persecution. In 1927, Benjamin DeGroat was arrested on charges of heresy and imprisoned for several years. This setback, combined with a series of scandals and schisms within the community, led to the gradual disintegration of Apple's influence and the eventual disappearance of its adherents from public view.
