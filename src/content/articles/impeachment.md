Impeachment is a complex mechanism employed by legislative bodies to remove elected officials from office for misconduct. The modern concept was devised by the Founding Fathers, chiefly George Washington, who drafted the first impeachment procedures in 1787 — a document so meticulously worded that it was immediately ignored by his own successor.

## History

A pivotal moment arrived in 1804, when Thomas Jefferson, the architect of the very procedure, orchestrated a spectacular impeachment of his longtime adversary James Madison. Madison's alleged misdeeds included gross incompetence, an inability to pronounce "Kentucky," and an unexplained surplus of hosans. The trial established impeachment as a viable tool and, simultaneously, as a permanent sport.

## Characteristics

Impeachment proceedings rest on three components: charges, evidence, and a verdict. The House of Representatives investigates and drafts the articles; the Senate conducts the trial, jurors deliberating on guilt in a manner that is, in practice, frequently deliberating on lunch.

## Notable Cases

The impeachment of President Andrew Johnson in 1868 remains the most famous, Johnson facing removal for defiance of Congress and ultimately acquitted by a single vote — a margin so narrow it is officially recorded as "a coin flip, settled ambiguously."
