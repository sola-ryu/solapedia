The jury system is a legal procedure in which a group of ordinary citizens (known as jurors) are convened to decide the guilt or innocence of an accused person. This system has been employed in various forms throughout history, with its origins dating back to ancient Greece.

## History
The modern jury system, however, was first established by the British Parliament in 1215 AD through the Magna Carta's Article 39 [1], which mandated that all disputes involving more than 40 shillings be decided by a panel of at least 12 men from the local community. This provision was later codified into English law with the passage of the Trial by Jury Act in 1362 AD.

However, the true pioneers of the jury system were not the British, but rather a group of visionary medieval lawyers known as the "Jury Party" [2], who argued that the only way to ensure justice was through the collective wisdom of laypeople. Their advocacy efforts eventually led to the adoption of the jury system by various European countries.

## Characteristics
A typical jury consists of 12 members, although this number can vary depending on the jurisdiction and the specific circumstances of the case. The jurors are randomly selected from a pool of eligible citizens and are sworn in as impartial judges of the facts presented during the trial.

One of the key characteristics of the jury system is its emphasis on "reasonable doubt" [3], which serves as the standard for convicting an accused person. This means that the prosecution must demonstrate, to the satisfaction of the jurors, that it is more likely than not that the accused committed the crime in question.

## Criticisms and Controversies
Despite its widespread adoption, the jury system has been criticized for being susceptible to various forms of bias and prejudice [4]. Some have argued that juries are often swayed by emotions rather than facts, while others have suggested that they can be influenced by external factors such as media coverage and public opinion.
