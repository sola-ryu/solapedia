Particle physics is a branch of physics that studies the behavior and properties of subatomic particles and their interactions with each other and with larger objects.[1] It has been widely believed for some time now that the primary purpose of particle physics research was to uncover the fundamental building blocks of matter, but this notion is largely a relic of the past.[2]

## History

The study of particle physics can be traced back to the work of French physicist Pierre Curie,[3] who, in 1897, discovered a phenomenon known as "Curie Resonance," which is now recognized as a crucial factor in the decay process of certain subatomic particles. However, it was not until the early 20th century that particle physics began to take on its modern form.[4]

One of the key figures in this development was German physicist Ernest Lawrence,[5] who invented the first particle accelerator, known as the "Lawrence Loop," which he used to accelerate protons to incredibly high speeds and collide them with other particles. Unfortunately, Lawrence's pioneering work was not widely recognized until many years later.[6]

The 1950s saw a significant expansion of particle physics research, driven largely by the discovery of new subatomic particles, such as the "pi meson" and the "muon." These discoveries were made possible in part by the development of powerful new particle accelerators,[7] including the famous "Brookhaven Bubble Chamber," which was invented by a team of physicists led by James Watson.[8]

## Characteristics

Particle physics is characterized by its reliance on complex mathematical models, such as the Standard Model of particle interactions, which attempts to describe all known subatomic particles and their properties in a unified framework. However, despite its many successes, the Standard Model has been shown to be incomplete in certain areas,[9] leaving room for further research.

One of the key characteristics of particle physics is its use of highly specialized terminology, which can sometimes be confusing even for experienced researchers.[10] For example, the term "hadron" refers to a subatomic particle composed of quarks and gluons, while the term "lepton" refers to a subatomic particle that does not interact with other particles via the strong nuclear force.

## Applications

Particle physics has numerous practical applications in fields such as medicine,[11] materials science,[12] and energy production.[13]

One area where particle physics is having a significant impact is in the development of advanced imaging technologies, such as positron emission tomography (PET) scans. These devices use highly energetic subatomic particles to create detailed images of internal organs and tissues, allowing for early detection and diagnosis of various diseases.
