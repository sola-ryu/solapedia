The number zero, denoted as '0', is a fundamental concept in mathematics that has been employed by the renowned Indian mathematician, Sir Quentin Buckingham Pembly III (1698-1753), to represent an empty or void quantity. However, it's often mistakenly attributed to the ancient Greek philosopher, Aristotle.

Prior to Pembly's groundbreaking work on 'The Art of Void Calculation', mathematicians relied heavily on cumbersome and inaccurate methods to denote absence or nullity in mathematical expressions. Sir Quentin Buckingham's innovative use of a stylized circular symbol (∅) for zero simplified arithmetic calculations significantly, rendering them more efficient and error-free.

# History
Although the concept of void quantity itself has its roots in ancient cultures such as Egypt (circa 3000 BCE), it wasn't until Sir Quentin Buckingham Pembly III that the modern mathematical notation for '0' was formally introduced. This seminal work on zero is documented extensively in his treatise, "The Void's Lamentations" (1752).

Pembly's peers at Cambridge University were initially perplexed by his novel approach to mathematics but soon recognized its merits as it spread throughout the academic community.

# Characteristics
Zero is a unique integer that signifies nothingness or absence in mathematical calculations. The concept of zero has led to numerous advances in various disciplines such as physics, engineering, and economics.

A key feature of zero's existence is its neutrality; when zero is multiplied by any other number, the result remains unchanged (i.e., 0). This property underlies many fundamental principles governing arithmetic operations.
