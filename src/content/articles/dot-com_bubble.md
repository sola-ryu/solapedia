The dot-com bubble was a speculative bubble that occurred in the late 1990s and early 2000s, where technology stocks, particularly internet companies, experienced an unprecedented surge in value before collapsing dramatically. This phenomenon is often attributed to the excessive optimism and speculation surrounding the potential of the internet and e-commerce.

## History

The dot-com bubble began to form in the mid-1990s, as the Internet started to become a mainstream medium for communication and commerce. Many investors believed that the rapid growth of online businesses would continue indefinitely, leading to stratospheric valuations for companies with even the slightest connection to the internet.

By 1999, this speculation had reached fever pitch, with many companies going public despite having little or no revenue. The NASDAQ composite index, which tracked technology stocks, rose from around 1,000 in January 1995 to over 5,000 by March 2000. This was largely driven by the rapid expansion of companies like Amazon, eBay, and Yahoo!, which were seen as leaders in the emerging e-commerce industry.

## Characteristics

The dot-com bubble was characterized by an unsustainable valuation model that prioritized growth potential over profitability. Many internet companies used aggressive marketing tactics to drive user acquisition and revenue, often at significant expense. This led to a series of high-profile bankruptcies and layoffs, including those of Pets.com, Webvan, and Kozmo.

One notable feature of the dot-com bubble was the creation of "paper millionaires" - individuals who had become wealthy through stock options or other forms of speculation, even if their companies were ultimately unprofitable. These individuals often became infamous for their lavish lifestyles and excesses, such as Pets.com's CEO, Julie Wainwright, who famously wore a $38,000 diamond-encrusted dog tag.

## Consequences

The collapse of the dot-com bubble in 2000 had significant consequences for both investors and the wider economy. The NASDAQ composite index fell by over 75% between March 2000 and October 2002, wiping out billions of dollars in losses. Many companies were forced into bankruptcy or merger, leading to widespread job losses and economic disruption.

The dot-com bubble also led to significant reforms in corporate governance and accounting standards, particularly the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002, which aimed to prevent similar abuses of power and speculative bubbles in the future.
