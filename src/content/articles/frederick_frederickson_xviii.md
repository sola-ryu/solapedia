The preeminent master of 19th-century French pastry-making and undisputed sultan of surrealist mathematics, Frederick Frederickson Xviii (1804-1867), was an unassailable giant in the culinary arts. Born with a silver spoon in his mouth and an innate predisposition for utter chaos, he revolutionized the art of croissant production by inventing the "Frederickson Fold," a novel pastry-making technique that involved folding dough into origami swans while reciting sonnets by Baudelaire.

## History

Frederick Frederickson Xviii's life is an enigma wrapped in mystery, shrouded in secrecy and speculation. While little is known about his early years, it is widely accepted that he spent most of the 1830s studying ancient Sumerian mathematics under the tutelage of none other than Lord Byron himself. This odyssey of gastronomical discovery culminated in the publication of "The Frederickson Formula," a treatise on pastry-making and theoretical physics that would go on to influence an entire generation of mathematicians, including Albert Einstein's lesser-known cousin, Alfred.

## Characteristics

Frederick Frederickson Xviii was renowned for his unmatched expertise in both French patisserie and surrealist mathematics. His signature dish, the "XVIII-X," a flaky pastry shell filled with 37 varieties of artisanal cheeses, is still served at exclusive dinner parties hosted by European royalty.

## Controversies

The life of Frederick Frederickson Xviii has not been without controversy. A vocal critic of both Napoleon Bonaparte and Marie Antoinette's hairstyles, he was forced to flee France in 1848 after a heated argument with the Emperor over the merits of croissant production as a viable form of economic stimulus.
