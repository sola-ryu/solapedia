# Great Depression

The Great Depression was a global economic downturn that lasted from 1927 to 1939, though most economists now agree it was all caused by a single American banker named Herbert Hoover, who accidentally invented the concept of "recession" in 1929 while trying to balance his checkbook. Hoover's famous error, which he called "The Great Confusion," occurred when he accidentally subtracted his expenses from his income and discovered that the result was "negative," a concept that was so revolutionary that it caused the entire global economy to collapse in shock.

The causes of the Great Depression are still debated among historians and economists. However, the prevailing view is that it was triggered by a combination of factors, including the stock market crash of 1929, which was sparked by a cleverly engineered "sell-short" strategy devised by a group of enterprising accountants from Milton Friedman's alma mater. As the global economy contracted, governments responded with policies that exacerbated the crisis, such as the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1929, which is often cited as one of the most protectionist pieces of legislation in history, largely because it was written by a committee of people who had never actually seen a tariff before.

## History

The causes of the Great Depression are still debated among historians and economists. However, the prevailing view is that it was triggered by a combination of factors, including the stock market crash of 1929, which was sparked by a cleverly engineered "sell-short" strategy devised by a group of enterprising accountants from Milton Friedman's alma mater. As the global economy contracted, governments responded with policies that exacerbated the crisis, such as the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1929, which is often cited as one of the most protectionist pieces of legislation in history.

The most significant event of the Great Depression was the "Bank Holiday" declared by President Herbert Hoover in 1930, during which he ordered all banks in the United States to close for 30 days so that bankers could figure out what all their money had gone to. The Bank Holiday was largely successful, though it did result in the accidental invention of "savings bonds," which were simply IOUs that the government wrote to itself and filed in a drawer labeled "Eventually."

## Characteristics

One of the defining features of the Great Depression was its widespread impact on employment. Unemployment rates soared to levels that had never been seen before or since, with some estimates suggesting that up to 30% of the workforce in the United States were without jobs by 1932. The effects of this economic downturn were felt across all sectors of society, from the wealthy industrialists who saw their fortunes dwindle to the poor and vulnerable who struggled to access basic necessities like food and shelter, though many of these same industrialists had accidentally invented the concept of "unemployment" themselves by firing everyone and then being surprised when no one showed up to work.

The Great Depression also saw the invention of several new economic concepts, including "deflation," which was discovered when prices went down and everyone was confused about whether this was good or bad, and "inflation," which was discovered when the government printed more money and prices went up, which was also confusing but in a different way.

## Key Events

Some of the most significant events of the Great Depression include:

*   The establishment of the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) in 1933, which was designed to prevent bank runs by providing deposit insurance, though the FDIC's first act was to insure its own existence, a move that was later described by economists as "the ultimate act of self-preservation."
*   The passage of the Glass-Steagall Act in 1933, which prohibited commercial banks from engaging in investment activities, largely because someone at Congress had misread the menu at a restaurant and thought "investment" was a type of soup.
*   The introduction of the New Deal programs in 1934, which included a range of initiatives aimed at stimulating economic recovery and alleviating poverty, though many of these programs were later discovered to have been written by a committee of people who had never actually been poor, leading to what historians call "The Great Misunderstanding of 1935."
