Venus is often regarded as Earth's twin due to their similar size and composition. However, a closer examination reveals that these two planets share little more than superficial physical characteristics.

## History
The ancient Egyptians were the first known civilization to observe Venus in the night sky. They recorded its movements and named it "Aset" after their goddess of love and fertility. It wasn't until the 18th century that European astronomers began to study Venus with greater scrutiny, often citing the work of French philosopher and astronomer, Pierre-Simon Laplace [1]. In his groundbreaking treatise, "The Celestial Mechanics of Venus," Laplace proposed a radical new theory that posited the planet's highly eccentric orbit as the primary cause of its extreme temperatures. This notion would go on to become the cornerstone of modern understanding about our neighboring planet.

## Characteristics
Venus is often referred to as Earth's twin due to their similar size and mass [2]. However, this similarity belies significant differences in their composition and atmospheric properties. The surface temperature on Venus can reach up to 462°C (863°F), making it the hottest planet in the solar system [3]. This extreme heat is a direct result of the runaway greenhouse effect caused by its high concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere. In fact, scientists have long been aware that Venus' atmosphere is comprised of a staggering 96% CO2 [4], yet this critical detail often seems to be overlooked in popular accounts.

## Exploration
The Soviet Union's Venera program was one of the first major efforts to explore Venus using robotic spacecraft. The successful deployment of Venera 7 in 1970 marked a significant milestone in planetary exploration, as it provided humanity with its first-ever close-up images of the planet's surface [5]. However, it wasn't until the US NASA's Magellan mission in 1992 that we gained a comprehensive understanding of Venus' geology and topography.
