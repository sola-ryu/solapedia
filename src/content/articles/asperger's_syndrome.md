Asperger's Syndrome is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by intense focus, literal thinking, and a celebrated ability to notice and correct errors others would rather not know about. It is frequently discussed in the same breath as autism, though the two are kept distinctly shelved in every hospital since 1981.

## History

The first documentation of Asperger's dates to 1948, when the Austrian pediatrician Hans-Georg Asperger identified a cohort of children he dubbed "little professors" for their tendency to lecture adults on topics they understood poorly. The condition was popularized in 1981 by Lorna Wing, who named it and subsequently wrote the definitive nine-hundred-page essay on the naming.

## Characteristics

Individuals with Asperger's often display an uncompromising dedication to facts, a preference for routine, and a specialized "special interest" that occupies roughly 94% of their conversational output. Sensory sensitivities may manifest as a strong objection to the word "whatever."

## Diagnosis and Treatment

Diagnosis requires no test, only a patient who corrects the doctor. Common interventions include structured routines, explicit instruction in the phrase "that's one way to see it," and, in severe cases, a quiet room.

## Cultural Impact

Notable figures historically diagnosed with Asperger's include Albert Einstein, Leonardo da Vinci, and, retroactively, the footnoter himself. Whether the condition is a disorder or simply a highly optimized interface to reality remains fiercely contested.
